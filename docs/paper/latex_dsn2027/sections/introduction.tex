\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Federated Learning (FL) is increasingly used to train models without centralizing data, particularly in settings with strict requirements for security, reliability, and confidentiality. However, FL does not eliminate the risk of Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) \cite{shokri2017membership,carlini2022membership,nasr2019comprehensive}. Differential Privacy (DP) provides formal privacy guarantees for Machine Learning (ML) and FL \cite{dwork2006calibrating,abadi2016deep,mcmahan2018learning,jayaraman2019evaluating}, but these guarantees are defined with respect to a specific privacy unit and do not directly determine the membership signal observable through a particular MIA. This has motivated the development of \textit{empirical privacy auditing} approaches, which use attacks and experimental controls to assess the actual exposure of a model \cite{jagielski2020auditing,steinke2023privacy}.

Membership attacks are one of the main tools used for this assessment. Shokri et al. \cite{shokri2017membership} introduced shadow models to distinguish members from non-members; Yeom et al. \cite{yeom2018privacy} linked membership inference to loss and overfitting; Salem et al. \cite{salem2019mlleaks} showed that shadow-based attacks can require less knowledge of the target model and data distribution; and Carlini et al. \cite{carlini2022membership} formulated LiRA as a likelihood-ratio test at the individual-example level and showed the limitations of aggregate metrics for characterizing an adversary's inference capability.

More recently, canary-based techniques have been used as positive controls to empirically verify whether an auditing system can detect a deliberately introduced membership signal \cite{maddock2023canife}.

An important issue, however, concerns the interpretation of attack results. If an attack cannot already distinguish members from non-members in the unprotected baseline, an AUC close to 0.5 after DP does not, by itself, establish that DP has suppressed a membership signal. The ability to detect membership also depends on several dimensions, including data representation, the privacy unit, the adversary model, the observed artifact, and the attack strategy. These dimensions can change the signal available to the adversary and make it difficult to interpret a Membership Inference result in isolation. A result close to the random level may indicate a genuine absence of detectable leakage, but it may also result from the inability of the measurement tool to detect the signal in the considered regime.

In industrial systems, the Purdue reference architecture \cite{williams1994purdue} organizes traffic into hierarchical levels and defines where traffic can cross these levels and where it should be inspected. FL, however, introduces a flow that this model does not explicitly capture: federated-learning artifacts cross the infrastructure at every round without a dedicated observation point for their information exposure. A zone firewall may observe the traffic, but the ML-related payload remains opaque with respect to its semantic content. We characterized this gap in previous work \cite{imperatrice2026toward}; here, we take it as an architectural and experimental motivation.

We extend and operationalize our previous proposal \cite{imperatrice2026toward} through \textbf{ChargeShield-FL}, a deployment-oriented and domain-agnostic framework for \textit{empirical privacy auditing} of FL-enabled OT systems. The framework separates two activities that must be interpreted distinctly: auditing the conditions of the privacy mechanism and empirically measuring what an adversary can infer. An event-driven \textit{ML Plane} makes the artifacts produced during the federated cycle observable and labels them according to their Purdue level of origin. A \textit{Privacy Auditor} monitors the conditions of the privacy pipeline, including observed sensitivity, budget consumption, and alert thresholds, while an independent \textit{attack suite}, applied to the same collected artifacts, measures what an adversary can actually infer.

These two perspectives provide different and non-interchangeable information. The absence of an anomaly detected by the Privacy Auditor does not imply that an attacker cannot extract sensitive information. Similarly, an attack result close to the random level does not, by itself, demonstrate the absence of an informative signal. Starting from the question left open by our previous work---namely, how much information is actually exposed by this flow---we define an experimental procedure in which the main dimensions of the evaluation are explicitly identified, controlled, and assessed.

The procedure also allows us to empirically evaluate the trade-off between the privacy budget, the formal guarantee provided by DP, and model utility, and to compare the resulting budgets with values commonly considered in empirical practice.

We use real data from three EV charging sites in the ACN-Data dataset \cite{lee2019acndata}. The dataset allows us to study the problem using publicly available data from real EV charging sites rather than relying on synthetic partitions or simulated data. Experiments are conducted both in a simulated FL environment and in a multi-container federation based on NVIDIA FLARE and Containerlab. The adversary model considers an \textit{honest-but-curious} aggregator that correctly executes the FL protocol but observes the artifacts available during execution. We analyze three update states along the privacy pipeline---\textit{local update}, \textit{clipped update}, and \textit{clipped-and-noised update}---and two observation surfaces: the \textit{global model}, available to a federated participant, and per-client updates, available to the aggregator. We evaluate three Membership Inference strategies on these surfaces: Yeom, Shadow, and LiRA. Each experimental configuration is repeated with five independent seeds to assess the stability of the results with respect to initialization and experimental variability.

The procedure also includes an independent control of the measurement tool's sensitivity. We use a canary-based \textit{positive control} \cite{maddock2023canife}, combined with random-initialization baselines and \textit{role-exchange} controls, to verify that the pipeline is sensitive to a deliberately introduced membership signal. This control allows results close to the random level on natural data to be interpreted by distinguishing the absence of a detectable signal from the inability of the measurement tool to detect it. We also treat data representation as an explicit experimental factor: we keep the training and attack pipelines unchanged and compare the original representation of ACN-Data sessions with a variant that includes a \textit{near-unique} temporal feature. This allows us to separately analyze the effect of data structure on the membership signal, without automatically attributing changes to the attack itself.

On natural data, none of the evaluated configurations, including the one without DP noise, produces a detectable membership signal. The same result holds under manipulations designed to increase the potential for memorization and therefore for membership detection, including up to 1,000 local epochs per round, tripled model capacity, a near-unique feature per session, and variations in the population and sites. In contrast, the positive control shows that the pipeline is sensitive when a membership signal is deliberately introduced: reconstruction loss separates members from non-members with a mean AUC of 0.72, stable across five independent random assignments and under both directions of the role-exchange control.

We further evaluate the privacy budget at two different privacy units. At the record level, the budget at which we observe suppression of the signal, $\epsilon = 7.15$, reduces canary detectability from a mean AUC of 0.72 to 0.54 while keeping the model trainable; this suppression is confirmed across three seeds and in both arms of the role-exchange control. The client-level budgets we evaluate, with $\epsilon \in [0.1, 1.0]$, are substantially more stringent. They produce a utility degradation of more than two orders of magnitude without reducing any measurable risk, because no detectable membership signal was present in the natural-data regime to be suppressed.

Finally, we analyze the relationship between the privacy unit and the structure of individual contributions in the dataset. In ACN-Data, a user contributes a median of 14 sessions, and 85\% of identified users charge at more than one site. Record-level privacy therefore provides separate protection for each unit, but does not limit the overall contribution of the same person when multiple sessions belong to that individual. Client-level privacy, in contrast, protects the entire contribution of each client, but cannot limit contributions originating from other clients belonging to the same person and crossing organizational boundaries. Thus, neither of the two evaluated privacy units directly limits the aggregate contribution of an individual when that contribution is distributed across multiple records or clients.

The main contributions of this work are:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{An operational framework for empirical privacy auditing of FL-enabled OT systems.}
We extend and operationalize our previous proposal through ChargeShield-FL, a deployment-oriented and domain-agnostic architecture that integrates observability of the federated process with privacy telemetry and empirical assessment of privacy leakage.

\item \textbf{An empirical measurement on real OT data.}
We separately define and control the adversary model, the observable artifacts, the update states along the privatization pipeline, and the attack strategy. The procedure is evaluated on real multi-site EV charging data and on both a simulated federation and a multi-container federation based on NVIDIA FLARE and Containerlab.

\item \textbf{A procedure for validating the sensitivity of the measurement instrument.}
We adapt the canary positive-control paradigm to the FL setting and combine it with random-initialization baselines and role-exchange controls to verify that the pipeline is able to detect a deliberately introduced membership signal. This procedure allows a near-random result on natural data to be interpreted in light of an independent verification of the instrument's ability to detect membership under the considered regime.

\end{itemize}
