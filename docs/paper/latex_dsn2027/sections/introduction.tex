\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Federated Learning (FL) is increasingly used to train models without centralizing data, particularly in settings with stringent security, reliability, and privacy requirements. However, FL does not eliminate the risk of Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) \cite{shokri2017membership,carlini2022membership,nasr2019comprehensive}. Differential Privacy (DP) provides formal privacy guarantees in Machine Learning (ML) and FL \cite{dwork2006calibrating,abadi2016deep,mcmahan2018learning,jayaraman2019evaluating}, but these guarantees are defined with respect to a specific privacy unit and do not directly determine the leakage observable through a particular MIA. This has motivated the development of \textit{empirical privacy auditing} approaches, which use attacks and experimental controls to assess the actual exposure of a model \cite{jagielski2020auditing,steinke2023privacy}. MIAs are among the main tools used for this type of evaluation. Shokri et al. \cite{shokri2017membership} introduced shadow models to distinguish members from non-members; Yeom et al. \cite{yeom2018privacy} linked membership inference to loss and overfitting; Salem et al. \cite{salem2019mlleaks} showed that shadow-based attacks can be made less dependent on knowledge of the model and training distribution; and Carlini et al. \cite{carlini2022membership} formulated LiRA as a likelihood-ratio test at the individual-example level and highlighted the limitations of aggregate metrics for characterizing an attacker's inference capability. More recently, canary-based techniques have been used as positive controls to empirically assess whether a privacy auditing system can detect a deliberately introduced membership signal \cite{maddock2023canife}.

Despite these results, empirical privacy evaluation in FL systems is affected by several dimensions, including data representation, privacy unit, adversary model, observed artifact, and attack strategy. These dimensions can affect the membership signal and make isolated MIA results difficult to interpret. In particular, a result close to the random level may indicate a genuine absence of leakage, but it may also result from the inability of the measurement tool to detect the signal in the considered regime.

In this work, we address this problem through a defined experimental procedure in which these dimensions are explicitly identified, controlled, and evaluated. We extend and operationalize our previous proposal \cite{imperatrice2026toward} through \textbf{ChargeShield-FL}, a deployment-oriented and domain-agnostic framework for empirical privacy auditing of FL-enabled OT systems. The framework separates the monitoring of privacy conditions from the empirical measurement of leakage: an event-driven ML Plane makes the artifacts produced during the federated cycle observable, while a Privacy Auditor monitors the conditions of the privacy pipeline and an independent attack suite measures what an adversary can actually infer.

We use real data from three EV charging sites in the ACN-Data dataset \cite{lee2019acndata}. Experiments are conducted both in a simulated FL environment and in a multi-container federation based on NVIDIA FLARE and Containerlab. The adversary model considers an \textit{honest-but-curious} aggregator that correctly executes the FL protocol but observes the artifacts available during execution. We analyze three update states along the privacy pipeline --- \textit{local update}, \textit{clipped update}, and \textit{clipped-and-noised update} --- and two observation surfaces: the \textit{global model}, available to a federated participant, and per-client updates, available to the aggregator. We evaluate three Membership Inference strategies on these surfaces: Yeom, Shadow, and LiRA.

The procedure also includes an independent control of the measurement tool's sensitivity. We adopt a canary-based positive control \cite{maddock2023canife}, combined with random-initialization baselines and role-exchange controls, to verify that the pipeline is sensitive to a deliberately introduced membership signal. We also treat data representation as an explicit experimental factor: we keep the training and attack pipeline unchanged and compare the original representation of ACN-Data sessions with a variant that includes a near-unique temporal feature. This allows us to separately analyze the effect of data structure on the membership signal without automatically attributing changes to the attack itself. On natural data, none of the evaluated configurations, including the one without DP noise, produces a detectable membership signal (TO BE VERIFIED AT THE END OF THE EXPERIMENTS), while the positive control confirms that the pipeline can detect membership when a signal is deliberately introduced.

The main contributions of this work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An operational framework for empirical privacy auditing of FL-enabled OT systems.} We extend and operationalize our previous proposal through ChargeShield-FL, a deployment-oriented and domain-agnostic framework that integrates observability of the federated process with privacy telemetry and empirical leakage assessment.

\item \textbf{A systematic procedure for multi-surface and multi-attack privacy exposure evaluation.} We separately define and control the adversary model, observable artifacts, update states along the privacy pipeline, and attack strategy.

\item \textbf{A procedure to validate the sensitivity of the measurement tool.} We adapt the canary positive-control paradigm to the FL setting and combine it with random-initialization baselines and role-exchange controls to verify that the pipeline can detect a deliberately introduced membership signal. This procedure allows results close to the random level on natural data to be interpreted in light of an independent verification of the tool's ability to detect membership in the considered regime.

\end{itemize}
