\begin{abstract}
Charging-session records tell where and when a person charges a vehicle, and
repositories holding them, together with names, vehicle identifiers and
protocol logs, have been exposed or stolen repeatedly in recent years.
Federated learning (FL) avoids pooling these records, but the artifacts it
shares can still reveal whether a given session was used for training, and
differential privacy (DP) bounds that leakage only for a chosen protected
unit and mechanism. We present an empirical study of membership inference and
reconstruction cost in federated autoencoders trained on 66{,}713 real
sessions from the three ACN-Data sites, one client per site. We ask how attack
success and model cost change with the DP configuration and the training
length, with the federated algorithm (FedAvg or FedProx), and with the data
partition, under client-level and record-level DP and three adversary
observation points, using loss-threshold, shadow-calibrated and
likelihood-ratio attacks, a per-record analysis and instrument controls. On
the evidence verified so far, without DP no attack separates members from
non-members on average (AUC $0.50$--$0.51$ over 49 runs), yet a per-record
analysis recognises a systematic excess of sessions over the chance level;
client-level DP at $\varepsilon \le 1$ removes that excess only while raising
the reconstruction error more than 100-fold, so in those cells protection and
loss of utility cannot be told apart. A canary positive control with role
exchange shows that the measurement detects induced memorisation on one site.

\end{abstract}
